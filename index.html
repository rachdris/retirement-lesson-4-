How much do I need to retire, and how do I get there?

The right amount is different for everyone. These two steps help you find your number and plan how to reach it.

  1. Step 1 Estimate your retirement number
  2. Step 2 Calculate how much to save each year to reach it

Step One How much will you need?

Start by asking yourself a few questions, then enter your answers into the calculator to find your estimate.

How much do you expect to spend in retirement?

Think about how much you would need each month to live comfortably in retirement. For example, if $5,000 a month ($60,000 a year) would cover your needs and wants, that’s your starting point.

Not sure? Start with what you spend now. Your estimate will get clearer as retirement gets closer.

If you’ll have other sources of income in retirement, like a pension or Social Security, subtract it from your expected spending. What’s left is the amount your savings will need to cover.

How long do you expect to be retired?

This depends on when you stop working and how long you live. Many people plan to retire at 65, and Americans who reach 65 can expect to live about 20 more years on average, according to the CDC. Many live longer, so planning for longer periods, 25 or 30 years, is reasonable.

If you’d like to retire sooner, or expect to live longer, you’ll have to cover more years.

How much do you expect your investments to grow in retirement?

Your expected return is an estimate of how much your savings will grow each year, and it depends on what you invest in.

In retirement, many people shift toward safer investments, like bonds, which typically come with lower returns. A 4% return is a common assumption.

However, it is important to account for inflation, which is the rise in prices over time.

The Federal Reserve aims for 2% annual inflation, though it has been higher in recent years. At 2%, prices double about every 35 years, so $5,000 a month in 35 years would only buy what $2,500 buys today.

To keep your number in today’s dollars without guessing future prices, simply subtract your expected inflation rate from your expected return.

Example: Putting it all together

Suppose you estimate you’ll need $60,000 a year to live comfortably in retirement. You want to retire a little early at 60 and plan to live to 90, a 30-year retirement.

You plan on holding your savings in safer investments that generate a return, say 4% before inflation. Subtracting the 2% target inflation rate, you plan on 2% growth.

Your target number: $1,343,787 With this saved in today’s dollars by age 60, you can spend what $60,000 buys today every year until 90, when your savings run out. If you ignored inflation and used 4%, your target would be $1,037,522 (about $300,000 less). In today’s dollars, that would only support about $46,000 a year, falling short of your $60,000 goal.
The math behind the number:
PV=Cr(1−1(1+r)T)
  • PV = the retirement number (what we’re solving for)
  • C = 60,000 (yearly retirement spending)
  • r = 0.02 (a 2% return after inflation)
  • T = 30 (years of retirement)

With the numbers filled in:

PV=60,0000.02(1−1(1.02)30)
  1. 60,000 ÷ 0.02 = 3,000,000
  2. (1.02)30 = 1.811362, and 1 ÷ 1.811362 = 0.552071
  3. 1 − 0.552071 = 0.447929
  4. 3,000,000 × 0.447929 = $1,343,787

Step Two How much should you save each year?

Now that you have your retirement target, you can figure out how much to save and invest each year to reach it. A few questions shape the answer:

How many years do you have until retirement?

The further away retirement is, the less you have to save each year due to compound growth, where you earn a return on both your contributions and your past earnings.

Waiting to save means you will have to save more each year to reach the same target.

How much do you expect your investments to grow while you work?

While saving for retirement, people often hold a larger share of higher-risk investments, like stocks. Historically, U.S. stocks have grown around 10% a year, before inflation, making 6% to 8% after inflation a common assumption for retirement savings invested mostly in stocks.

Your return depends on what you invest in (stocks, bonds, cash), how you diversify risk and what you pay in fees:

  • Diversifying, spreading your money across many investments, means one poor performer is less likely to derail your plan.
  • Fees add up over time and eat into your returns.

Does your employer provide matching contributions?

In many jobs, your employer will add money to your retirement account, but often only if you put in some of your own. This is called an employer match. For example, say your employer matches 100% of what you put in up to 5% of your pay. If you contribute 5%, your employer adds another 5%.

Your yearly savings amount stays the same. The match simply reduces how much you need to contribute yourself. Workplace retirement accounts go by different names, such as 401(k), 403(b), 457(b) or Thrift Savings Plan.

Example

Say you determined in Step One that you need $1,343,787 for retirement. You start saving at 25 and plan to retire at 60, so you have 35 years.

While you work, you expect a 9% annual return before inflation. Subtracting 2% inflation leaves 7%. To reach your target, you need to save:

Annual savings: $9,721 a year This is in today’s dollars. In practice, you would save $9,721 in your first year, then raise the amount by 2% each year to keep up with inflation, so every year you are setting aside the equivalent of $9,721 of today’s spending.
The math behind this answer:
FV=Cr((1+r)T−1)
  • FV = 1,343,787 (the retirement number from Step One)
  • C = the yearly savings (what we’re solving for)
  • r = 0.07 (a 7% return after inflation)
  • T = 35 (years until retirement)

With the numbers filled in:

1,343,787=C0.07((1.07)35−1)
  1. (1.07)35 = 10.676581, and 10.676581 − 1 = 9.676581
  2. 1,343,787 × 0.07 = 94,065.09
  3. 94,065.09 ÷ 9.676581 = $9,721
